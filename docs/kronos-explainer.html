Kronos

A foundation model that reads candlestick charts the way a language model reads text. Each price bar becomes a token, and the model learns to predict the next one.

Each candle (open, high, low, close, volume, amount) is compressed into one 20-bit token, shown here as two halves: a coarse 10-bit subtoken for the rough shape and a fine 10-bit subtoken for the detail. Hollow = closed up, filled = closed down. Illustrative data.

The paper in four lines

  • Problem. General time-series foundation models (TimesFM, Chronos, Moirai…) barely saw financial data in training and often lose to small models trained from scratch on market data.
  • Idea. Build a tokenizer for candlesticks, then train a GPT-style model on 12 billion of them from 45 exchanges.
  • Trick. Each token is split into a coarse half and a fine half. The model predicts the rough shape first, then fills in the detail.
  • Result. With zero task-specific training it beats 25 baselines on price, return and volatility forecasting, synthetic data generation and a stock-picking backtest.

Why a finance-only model

The big time-series models never really studied markets

Candlestick data (the paper calls it K-line data, the common term in Chinese markets) is noisy, non-stationary and heavy-tailed. Most of what happens in a bar is noise, the statistics drift over time, and rare crashes matter more than typical days. Generic forecasting models are built on assumptions that suit electricity load or weather, and they saw very little market data during training.

The authors confirm the consequence empirically. Zero-shot, general foundation models often do worse than iTransformer- or TimesNet-style models trained only on the target data. Kronos keeps the foundation-model recipe (pre-train once, use everywhere) and changes the diet: 100% candlesticks.

How it works

Discretize, then generate

Kronos has two separately trained parts: a tokenizer that turns continuous bars into discrete tokens and back, and a decoder-only Transformer that learns which token tends to come next. Prediction runs through five steps:

Normalize

Z-score each of the six columns (O, H, L, C, volume, amount) using the input window's own mean and std. Clip to ±5 so a flash crash can't blow up the scale.

Encode

A small Transformer encoder (3 layers, width 256) maps each bar to a latent vector, using context from neighbouring bars.

tokenizer

Quantize (BSQ)

Project the vector onto a unit sphere and keep only the sign of each of 20 dimensions. That gives 20 bits, split into a coarse 10 and a fine 10.

coarse 10b fine 10b

Predict

The main Transformer reads the token history plus time-of-day, weekday and month embeddings. It predicts the next coarse subtoken, then the fine one given the coarse.

autoregressive

Decode

The tokenizer's decoder turns generated tokens back into OHLCVA bars, and the normalization is undone to get real prices.

tokenizer

Step 3, up close: what one token holds

The tokenizer is trained with a hierarchical reconstruction loss. The decoder must rebuild the bar from the coarse bits alone (a blurry version), and again from all 20 bits (a sharp version). This forces the first 10 bits to capture the main shape and leaves the residual detail to the last 10. Pick a candle below to see the idea.

Why BSQ instead of a plain codebook

Binary Spherical Quantization comes from video tokenizers. Because vectors are normalized onto a sphere before taking signs, the quantization error has a hard upper bound, so one extreme outlier cannot produce an unbounded error. Codebook usage is high: 97.66% of coarse codes and 85.25% of fine codes get used. Rarely used tokens decode to extreme, long-wicked bars, which suggests the vocabulary is meaningful.

Why split 20 bits into 10 + 10

A single softmax over 220 classes needs about 1.7B parameters just for the embedding and output tables. Two softmaxes over 210 = 1,024 cut that to 3.4M. Splitting further saves almost nothing and adds a sequential decoding step for each extra piece.

Step 4, up close: coarse first, then fine

The model factorizes each step with the chain rule. The coarse head reads the Transformer's hidden state. The fine head uses cross-attention, with the embedding of the chosen coarse subtoken as the query and the history as keys and values.

P(bt | b<t) = P(btcoarse | b<t) · P(btfine | b<t, btcoarse)

One training detail is easy to miss. The fine head is conditioned on the coarse token the model sampled, not on the true one (no teacher forcing). At inference the model never sees the truth, so training it on its own guesses reduces exposure bias.

Step 5, up close: sample many futures, then average

Kronos generates the future like text: token by token, with a temperature and top-p. Because it is generative, you can run it N times and average the paths. The paper calls this test-time scaling: IC and RankIC rise steadily with more samples, at the cost of more compute and no retraining.

What it learned from

12.11 billion bars, heavy on the US and China

The corpus covers 96,569 instruments: stocks, ETFs, indices, crypto, futures and more than 1,000 forex pairs, at frequencies from 1-minute to weekly. Four venues (NASDAQ, NYSE, Shanghai, Shenzhen) account for about two-thirds of it, so the authors upweight crypto, futures and forex during training.

Cleaning pipeline

Split the series wherever price data is missing or jumps between bars past a per-frequency threshold (splits, rollovers). Cut out long runs of zero volume and long runs of an unchanged close (dead feeds). Drop segments shorter than a minimum length. Thresholds scale with frequency, for example a 10% gap on 1-minute bars versus 30% on daily bars.

Built to work without volume

During training, volume and amount are zeroed on 5% of samples at random, so the model learns to forecast from price alone. Crypto and forex tests deliberately feed OHLC only. In the repo, volume and amount are optional columns.

What it achieves

Five tasks, one model, no task-specific training

Kronos is tested zero-shot against 25 baselines: 8 models trained on the target data ("full-shot"), 5 general foundation-model families, ARCH/GARCH for volatility, and 3 generative models. Scores are averaged over 9 stock exchanges (3 never seen in pre-training), crypto and forex, at several frequencies.

How to read IC and RankIC: the correlation (Pearson, or Spearman on ranks) between forecasts and what actually happened. In finance, values of 0.02–0.07 count as real signal, because a small edge applied across thousands of assets and days adds up.

Scaling holds. On almost every task, large beats base beats small. That pattern, plus the vocabulary ablation (bigger vocab, better forecasts), is the paper's evidence that adding parameters and tokens keeps paying off for market data.

How to read these numbers

Strong research result, not a money printer

The best model isn't released

Kronos-large (499M) produced the headline numbers. The open weights are mini, small and base. Base still beats every baseline in the paper's averages, by a smaller margin.

Absolute signal is small

A price-series RankIC of 0.027 is about double the next-best model, but it's still a weak correlation. It's useful at portfolio scale and close to useless for calling one stock's next move.

One backtest market

The trading simulation is long-only top-k on Chinese A-shares (CSI 300/800) with 0.15% costs, and no risk neutralization, slippage model or capacity analysis. The authors say themselves it isn't production-ready.

Price and volume only

Kronos sees nothing but bars: no news, fundamentals, order book or cross-asset links within a forecast. Each series is forecast on its own.

Short memory

Context is 512 bars (2,048 for mini). For long horizons you switch to coarser bars, such as daily data for monthly views.

The mean hides the spread

The repo's predict() averages all sampled paths and returns only that mean. The distribution, which is the most useful output for risk work, is thrown away unless you change the code.

out = pred.predict(

    pred_len=120, T=0.6, top_p=0.9, sample_count=10)

What predict() actually does

  1. Checks for open/high/low/close. Fills missing volume with 0, and missing amount with volume × mean price.
  2. Turns timestamps into minute, hour, weekday, day and month features for the time embeddings.
  3. Z-scores the window with its own mean and std, then clips to ±5.
  4. Copies the window sample_count times and encodes each copy into (coarse, fine) token pairs.
  5. For each future step: decode_s1 → sample a coarse token → decode_s2 → sample a fine token. The context buffer rolls forward once it reaches 512.
  6. Decodes all tokens back to bars, averages across samples, undoes the z-score and returns a DataFrame.

Generation is a plain Python loop with no KV cache, so each step re-runs the whole context. Long horizons with many samples get slow. That's an easy speed-up to build.

What to build on top

Ideas, from a weekend to a research project

Kronos is most useful as a component. Treat it as a pre-trained engine that turns any OHLCV series into calibrated-ish futures, embeddings and a discrete vocabulary of market states. These ideas use those pieces directly.

Forecasts with uncertainty

Return every sampled path instead of the mean, then serve quantile bands, probability of finishing up, and Value-at-Risk / expected shortfall per asset. This is the most valuable output Kronos produces, and the repo currently discards it.

weekendsamplingrisk

Volatility early-warning

Volatility was Kronos's strongest result (9% lower error than GARCH). Run it nightly over a watchlist or portfolio, compare forecast realized vol with recent vol, and alert on expected regime jumps. Useful for position sizing and options context.

weekendvolatilityalerts

"Markets like now" pattern search

Use the tokenizer alone: encode years of bars into token sequences, index them (n-grams or vector search on embeddings), and query "when did the last 50 bars look like this, and what happened next?" It's fast, explainable and needs no generation.

couple of weekstokenizersearch

Anomaly detector from token rarity

The paper shows rare tokens decode to extreme bars. Score each new bar by how surprising its token is to the model (negative log-likelihood) to flag unusual moves, broken feeds or possible manipulation, with no labels needed.

couple of weekslikelihoodmonitoring

Synthetic market generator

Generate thousands of plausible alternative histories (T ≈ 1.0) to stress-test strategies, augment thin datasets, or train RL trading agents on more than one realized path. In the paper, models trained on Kronos-generated data scored about 2× better on real data than with the next-best generator.

couple of weeksgenerationbacktesting

Cross-sectional signal + portfolio layer

Rank a universe daily by Kronos's expected return, then add what the authors left out: factor neutralization, turnover limits, realistic costs and walk-forward evaluation. This turns a research signal into a strategy you can test honestly.

couple of weeksalphaquant

Forecast tool for an LLM agent

Wrap the predictor as an MCP server or API tool. An agent pulls bars, calls Kronos for paths and vol, and explains the result in plain language alongside news it can read. Kronos supplies the numbers it can't see, and the LLM supplies the context Kronos can't read.

weekendagentsMCP

Multimodal Kronos

Kronos is price-only. Fuse its hidden states with news/sentiment embeddings, fundamentals, or a cross-asset attention layer so one forecast can see related tickers. Fine-tune on top of the frozen backbone.

researchfusionfine-tune

Faster, cheaper inference

Add a KV cache to the generation loop, batch across tickers and samples, and export mini/small to ONNX or quantize for CPU and Apple-silicon serving. This makes N = 50 samples over a 500-stock universe affordable.

couple of weeksinfralatency
